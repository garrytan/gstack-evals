# CEO review (native voice, attempt a1): outcome test for every gstack skill

Reviewer: one strong CEO voice, Opus 5.5, on the shared GEVA-225 machine. Input file checked: sha256 42e28cee...7b0b, 65,700 bytes, matching the prompt. I read the whole plan, checked its arithmetic by hand, counted its skill map, read the crew rules it cites (CREW.md, org rules.md R65), and ran one web search for prior art.

## Verdict

This is the right problem, and the plan is unusually honest about how it could come out null. The rig reuse, the stand-in arm, intention-to-treat exposure and the admission gate are the right calls. The arithmetic mostly holds: every confirmatory cell and the $174,000 and $190,000 totals reproduce from section 8's per-trial prices within rounding. Three things need fixing before the gate. First, the G family's primary measure is biased against the very thing planning skills claim to do, because statements written from the merged change and hidden tests pinned to the maintainers' interfaces leave no room for a different design or scope. Second, the $174,000 confirmatory ask is framed as one fixed-size decision across nine threads when a staged or group-sequential design would answer most nulls at about half the cost. Third, the $190,000 total leaves out bulk authoring, which for families U and X is the largest human-and-model cost in the program.

## 1. Is this the right problem? Could a reframing give 10x?

Yes, it is the right problem: gstack loads planning and ship text on every run, and nobody has outcome evidence for any of it. The plan's own framing already holds the highest-value reframing, the told minus stand-in contrast, because that is the one that decides whether the skill's procedure text earns its load cost (R56's diet). I would make it more explicit that this contrast is the program's headline decision rule, and size for it first.

The bigger reframing risk is in what the G family can see. Tasks are real merged changes with statements written by an author who has read the gold change, and the hidden tests call the maintainers' interfaces (V10 even requires follow-up tests to use only interfaces at the base or named in the statement). So the agent gets a change whose design is already settled, and gets graded on whether it reproduced that design. autoplan's CEO phase (scope), its design phase and plan-eng-review's architecture pass can then only matter through a better test plan and fewer slips. Worse, an unattended /autoplan with a rec user that answers "Go with your recommendation" and "do it" will accept scope expansions, and any extra behavior that changes an interface or breaks the base suite counts as unsolved. The measure will read "planning hurts or does nothing" for a reason that is about the instrument. The plan handles this for spec and office-hours (G-v tasks) but not for autoplan and plan-eng-review, which are wave 1's headline skills (F1).

## 2. Premises: stated or assumed

- Stated and checked: future bugs are too scarce for a primary (the 105 links x 3.8% arithmetic gives about 0.25 a repository per four months, and the plan acts on it). Good.
- Assumed: that the after pin will be named in time. The confirmatory G prereg is dated 2026-11-27. If GST-1088's after pin is not named, the confirmatory told arm becomes the forced line on the before pin, a product nobody ships. A KEEP or CUT-CANDIDATE from that cell is a verdict on "the skill with its steps forced", which cannot justify cutting or keeping the shipped text by itself (F3).
- Assumed: per-trial prices. Every told price is scaled from small plan units, and autoplan's $12 is labelled unmeasured. The sensitivity line is the right instinct but is wrong (F5).
- Assumed: that 7 of 8 sampled pilot trials shows 80% exposure. A 7/8 sample has a 95% Clopper-Pearson lower bound of about 47%, so the admission row can pass a skill whose true exposure is near half (F9).
- Assumed: that U checkers judge outcomes, not layouts. Section 3 allows checkers on "a URL or DOM state". A checker written against the gold UI's URL or DOM fails a different but equally usable design (F8).

## 3. The six-month regret scenario

It is May 2027. Wave 1 spent about $190,000 plus an unbudgeted authoring bill. Opus G came back "not distinguished" for autoplan at 10.5 points, because the binding column at 120 x 4 never reached the 10 points decision 6 promised and planning's real effects were squeezed out by settled statements. U slipped two months because 360 web-app candidates with three hand-written scenarios each did not exist among permissive repositories. The told arms ran on the forced line at the before pin, and gstack had shipped twenty releases since. Meanwhile SkillsBench, a public benchmark with the same paired skill-lift protocol on Harbor, had already published per-skill lift numbers that people cited instead.

Each part of that is avoidable: stage the confirmatory, fix the G instrument for planning, price authoring, and position against prior art.

## 4. Alternatives dismissed without enough analysis

- **Staged or group-sequential confirmatory.** Decision 6 compares fixed sizes only. A design with one interim look at 60 tasks (futility and efficacy boundaries, O'Brien-Fleming style) keeps about the same power at 120 and stops clear nulls and clear wins near half the cost. Running Opus G first (about $14,000) before committing $160,000 to the other eight threads costs a few weeks and buys the most information per dollar. The plan names a 15-point screen as an option "at about 45% of the cost"; by its own table 60 x 4 is $87,000 of $174,000, which is 50% (F2, F4).
- **Interface-only statements for planning skills.** Instead of statements written from the merged change, give the agent the user need plus only the public interfaces the hidden tests call, so design inside that boundary is free. This is the obvious middle ground between G and G-v and is not discussed (F1).
- **Reusing SkillsBench's tasks or format.** SkillsBench (arXiv 2602.12670, benchflow-ai/skillsbench, skillsbench.ai) already runs paired no-skill versus skill conditions on Harbor and reports a paired skill lift. The plan does not cite it, compare against it or say why its tasks don't fit (F10).

## 5. Competitive risk

The general question, "do agent skills help", is already being answered in public by SkillsBench, on the same Harbor runner this rig uses. That is not a reason to stop: gstack-evals' value is outcome evidence on gstack's own skills, on real post-cutoff repository work, with a stand-in arm and an exposure gate, and SkillsBench (as far as I can tell from its public pages) has no stand-in contrast and no adherence gate. But the plan should say this in its own words, borrow what fits (task packaging, their reported lift as a prior for effect sizes), and check SkillsBench's licence if anything is reused. Being second with a better-controlled design is fine; being second without citing the first looks careless in a public repo.

## 7. R65: outside terms, licences and rights

Yes, outside terms are involved, and section 11 answers the question better than most plans. It names the repo licences, the tool licences, Claude Code's terms for non-Anthropic models, provider terms, particle-1's licence and the OpenRouter data-collection deny. What it misses:

- **Licence check depth.** V8 reads the SPDX id from the root LICENSE file only. Web-app repositories often carry more than one licence: an MIT root with an ee/ or enterprise directory under a commercial licence, vendored GPL code, or per-file SPDX headers. The check should scan every LICENSE, COPYING and NOTICE file and the SPDX headers in the paths a task touches and its hidden tests use. The permissive-only rule also shrinks the U pool a lot, because many active web apps are AGPL, which compounds the yield risk in section 14 (F11).
- **Issue and PR text is not under the code licence.** G-v tasks take "the user's need from the issue", and oracle summaries come from the merged behavior and its discussion. Issue and PR comments are user content under GitHub's terms, owned by their authors, not MIT or Apache text. Paraphrasing for internal sealed use is the right handling; the plan should say so explicitly and never publish or quote it (F12).
- **Claude Code driving Sol and particle-1.** The plan defers the clause to "the first prereg that runs Sol". That is the pilot prereg, so it must be settled before P-G, not at the confirmatory. Anthropic documents LLM-gateway use of Claude Code, so it is likely fine, but it has to be cited (F12).
- **Comparative publication and particle-1's licence.** Both are named as unverified. They gate any named results post, so make them a prereg checklist item, not a note (F12).
- **SkillsBench.** If any task, format or code is reused, its licence goes in NOTICE.md under the repo's vendored rule (F10).
- Handled well: the data-collection deny before sealed bodies go to OpenRouter, rewriting statements in our own words, counts and digests only in the repo, trademark naming deferred to the prereg.

## 8. Smaller points

- The exposure detector for autoplan requires "every phase autoplan's scope chose" to run in order; in an unattended session that also depends on the rec user's gate answer. Freeze that answer in the arm definition (the plan does) and also log whether the gate was reached.
- The plan targets O1 to O4 in 9 days. Crew history says /ship on outcome-classification code has taken multiple review rounds each. The dates are said to move with yield; say they also move with review.
- Section 13 calls the code PRs "scorer class under R65 and budget for a full /ship"; R65 as written is the ship-check diet (one review, one fix round), so "full /ship" should read as R65's printed plan for scorer-class code, not an extra panel.
